Commercial Backflow Testing: What Marietta Business Owners Need to Know

If your property has an irrigation system or certain commercial connections, annual testing likely isn't optional. Here's what to expect.

Backflow prevention is one of those requirements that business owners sometimes only learn about when a test lapses or a new device needs installing. If you own or manage commercial property in Cobb County, here's what's actually involved.

Why It's Required

Protecting the clean water supply

A backflow preventer stops contaminated water from siphoning back into the municipal clean water supply during a pressure drop, which can happen during firefighting activity, a water main break, or other unusual system conditions. Certain connections, irrigation systems and specific commercial fixtures among them, pose enough of a cross-contamination risk that Cobb County, like most jurisdictions, requires the device and annual testing to confirm it's functioning.

What Triggers the Requirement

Common situations that need a backflow preventer

Any irrigation system

Nearly universally required regardless of property type, residential or commercial.

Certain commercial kitchen connections

Food-service properties often have additional backflow requirements tied to their specific equipment.

Fire suppression systems

Sprinkler systems typically require their own backflow preventer given the connection's size and pressure characteristics.

Properties with auxiliary water sources

A well or other non-municipal water source on the same property as a municipal connection raises specific backflow concerns.

What the Test Actually Involves

A quick process, done annually

A certified tester checks that the device's check valves and relief mechanism function correctly under test conditions, using calibrated equipment. Most single-device tests take well under an hour. Results are typically submitted directly to the local water authority, either by the testing company or the property owner, depending on the specific jurisdiction's process.

What Happens If You Miss a Test

The consequences of letting it lapse

Consequences for a lapsed test vary by water authority, but they can include a compliance notice and, in some cases, water service consequences if the lapse continues. Real estate transactions and refinancing can also surface a lapsed test, since it sometimes comes up during a property inspection or a utility account transfer, adding friction to a process that's otherwise unrelated to your plumbing.

Warning Signs

When to have a device checked outside the annual cycle

Visible discharge from the device

Some designs discharge water when actively preventing backflow, but frequent or continuous discharge is worth having checked.

A noticeable drop in water pressure nearby

Can indicate a failing or partially stuck check valve within the device.

Discolored water near the connection point

A possible sign contamination has already occurred and the device isn't functioning as intended.

Any physical damage or tampering

A device that's been struck, exposed to freezing temperatures, or otherwise damaged should be checked before its next scheduled test, not left until then.

If you manage multiple properties or aren't sure whether your specific property has a backflow requirement, it's worth confirming rather than assuming. We handle this as part of our broader our commercial work work and can set up a testing schedule that keeps you ahead of it rather than reacting to a lapse notice.

Multiple Devices

Larger properties often have more than one

A larger commercial property sometimes has multiple backflow preventers, one for irrigation, another for a fire suppression system, and potentially others depending on the property's specific connections. Each device typically needs its own annual test, and keeping track of multiple devices with potentially different testing dates is a genuine administrative task worth organizing, whether that's through your own maintenance calendar or a service provider who tracks it for you.

New Installations

What changes when you add irrigation or new equipment

If you're installing a new irrigation system or adding commercial equipment that requires its own connection, a backflow preventer is typically required as part of that installation, tested before the connection is approved for use. This is worth planning for during the installation budget rather than discovering as an unexpected requirement partway through the project.

Whether you're managing one device or several, staying ahead of the testing schedule is a small, predictable cost compared to the disruption of a lapsed test surfacing during a property transaction or a compliance review.

Working With Property Managers

A recurring responsibility worth delegating clearly

For commercial properties managed by a property management company rather than an owner-occupant, it's worth clarifying upfront whose responsibility backflow testing compliance actually is, the owner's or the management company's, since ambiguity here is exactly how a test ends up lapsing. Building this into a standard property management agreement, along with other recurring compliance items, is a small step that prevents a larger compliance headache down the line.

Planning Ahead

Building testing into your annual budget

Treating backflow testing as a predictable annual line item, rather than a surprise expense each time it comes due, makes the whole process smoother. Most businesses find it easiest to schedule the test around the same time each year, tied to a lease renewal date or another recurring business milestone that's already on the calendar.

First-Time Installation

What new business owners should expect

If you're opening a new business and this is your first time dealing with a backflow requirement, know that it's a standard, well-understood part of commercial buildout rather than an unusual hurdle specific to your property. Most commercial general contractors are familiar with the requirement and typically build it into the initial plumbing installation and permitting process, so it shouldn't come as a surprise late in your buildout timeline if you're working with an experienced contractor.

If you're ever uncertain whether a specific connection on your property requires testing, it's worth asking rather than assuming either way, since both an unnecessary test and a skipped required one carry their own downsides.

A quick call is usually enough to get a clear answer, along with a sense of when your first or next test should be scheduled.
