Protecting Your Pipes During a Georgia Winter Freeze

Hard freezes are occasional here, not constant, which is exactly why so many Marietta homes aren't actually prepared for one.

5 min readMarietta, GA

Georgia winters are mild compared to most of the country, but that mildness is part of the problem. Because hard freezes are occasional rather than routine here, a lot of Marietta-area homes have pipe runs in crawl spaces and exterior walls that were never insulated the way they would be in a colder climate. When a real cold snap does arrive, those uninsulated sections are exactly the ones at risk.

Before the Freeze

What actually helps

Insulate exposed pipes in crawl spaces

Foam pipe insulation is inexpensive and takes a homeowner an afternoon to install on accessible sections.

Disconnect and drain garden hoses

A connected hose can cause water to back up and freeze in the exterior spigot itself.

Know where your main shutoff valve is

The single most useful thing to know before an emergency happens, not during one.

Seal gaps near pipe entry points

Cold air infiltration around where pipes enter the house through exterior walls adds real risk.

During a Hard Freeze

What to do while the cold snap is happening

Letting faucets drip slightly during a genuinely hard freeze relieves pressure in the line and keeps water moving, both of which reduce the odds of a freeze-related burst. Opening cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls lets warmer household air reach the pipes underneath. Neither of these is necessary for a typical mild Georgia winter night, but they're worth doing specifically during the occasional hard freeze warning.

If a Pipe Does Freeze

What to do, and what not to do

If you notice no water from a specific fixture during a cold snap, that's the clearest sign a line has frozen. Gentle, gradual heat, a hair dryer on an accessible section, or heat tape designed for the purpose, can often thaw it safely. What you don't want to do is use an open flame anywhere near a pipe. That's a genuine fire risk and can damage the pipe further rather than helping. Keep the affected faucet open while thawing so water and steam have somewhere to go as the ice melts.

If a frozen pipe has already burst, once discovered thawed or leaking, shutting off the water at the main valve immediately matters more than anything else you could do in the moment. From there, it becomes a straightforward repair rather than an emergency.

Looking Ahead

A little prevention goes a long way here

Because hard freezes are the exception rather than the rule in this area, it's easy to forget about pipe insulation until the week a freeze warning shows up. If you've been through a scare before, or if you know your home has exposed pipe in a crawlspace or garage, addressing it before next winter is a small project that prevents a much bigger fixing a burst section later, and we can pair an insulation check with a leak detection visit if you've had prior issues.

After the Fact

Documentation matters if you file a claim

If a pipe does burst despite reasonable precautions, homeowners insurance often covers at least the resulting water damage, though coverage for the pipe repair itself varies by policy. Taking a few photos of the damage and the burst location before cleanup begins gives you useful documentation regardless of how the claim ultimately gets handled. We can also provide a written summary of what we found and repaired, which is often exactly what an adjuster asks for.

One thing worth knowing: some policies include language about reasonable preventive steps, like maintaining heat in the home during cold weather, so keeping your thermostat at a reasonable minimum temperature during a hard freeze, even if you're away, is worth doing for insurance reasons as much as prevention itself.

Rental Properties

A note for landlords and property managers

If you manage a rental property rather than living in it full time, freeze prevention takes on extra importance since no one may notice a problem developing in real time. Confirming that tenants know where the main shutoff is, and that vacant properties maintain a minimum heat level during winter months, are both worth building into a standard seasonal checklist rather than leaving to chance.

One more consideration specific to this area: because hard freezes here are infrequent, it's easy to let preventive habits lapse between events. If it's been a few winters since your last real cold snap, that's actually the moment to double-check your insulation and preparation, not a reason to assume you're already covered. The gap between freeze events is exactly when preventive maintenance tends to get forgotten.

If you're planning a vacation or extended time away during winter months, it's worth setting your thermostat no lower than the mid-50s Fahrenheit rather than turning heat off entirely, even if the house will be empty. The cost difference in heating bills is small compared to the cost of a burst pipe discovered only when you return home, sometimes days after it happened.

Homes with a history of freeze-related issues in a specific location are also worth a closer look at insulation in that exact spot, since a pipe that's frozen once, in the same uninsulated section, is statistically likely to do so again during the next hard freeze unless something changes about that specific run. A small, one-time investment in insulation for that section is almost always cheaper than repeating the same emergency repair every few winters.
