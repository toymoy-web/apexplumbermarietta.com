Sewer Line Root Intrusion: Signs and Solutions for Older Marietta Homes

Mature trees and aging sewer laterals are a genuinely common combination across our older neighborhoods. Here's how to spot it early.

6 min readMarietta, GA

Root intrusion into a sewer line isn't a rare or unusual problem, it's one of the more common causes of a recurring sewer issue in Marietta's older, established neighborhoods. The combination that creates it is simple: mature trees, often decades old, planted near clay or early PVC sewer laterals that are roughly the same age as the trees themselves. Over enough time, root systems find their way into small cracks or joints in the pipe, and what starts as a minor intrusion gradually grows into a real blockage.

Why It Happens Here Specifically

Mature landscaping meets aging infrastructure

Neighborhoods like East Cobb's established subdivisions, built out in the 1970s through 1990s, often have large, mature trees that were planted around the same time as the homes themselves. Those trees have now had decades to develop extensive root systems, and roots are drawn toward the moisture and nutrients present in a sewer line, particularly at joints or any existing crack where they can gain entry.

The Warning Signs

What to watch for before it becomes a full backup

A recurring clog in the same drain

A clog that returns within weeks of snaking, rather than a one-time event, is the clearest sign of a structural issue rather than a fresh blockage.

Gurgling sounds from drains

Air trying to escape past a partial blockage, often noticeable when running the washing machine or dishwasher.

Unusually lush or green patches of lawn

A leaking sewer line acts as fertilizer for the grass directly above it, a genuinely reliable visual sign.

Sewage odor in the yard

Can indicate a crack that's leaking before it ever progresses to a full indoor backup.

How We Confirm It

A camera inspection removes the guesswork

Symptoms alone can't fully distinguish root intrusion from other causes of a recurring clog, like scale buildup or a bellied section of pipe. A sewer camera inspection shows the actual condition of the line, whether roots have entered at a specific joint, how extensive the intrusion is, and whether the pipe itself has sustained any structural damage from the roots' growth.

Fixing It

Options depend on how far it's progressed

For root intrusion caught relatively early, hydro jetting is often the right first step. The specialized root-cutting nozzles combine cutting action with high-pressure water, clearing the roots without needing to excavate the line. If the camera inspection reveals more significant structural damage, cracking or misalignment beyond what jetting can resolve, trenchless lining or a targeted spot repair becomes the better option, all performed to the Georgia State Minimum Standard Plumbing Code (IPC-based). Full excavation and replacement is generally reserved for a line with damage extending across most of its length, which is less common than a localized intrusion point.

If you're dealing with a clog that keeps coming back no matter how many times it's been snaked, that pattern itself is worth paying attention to. A camera inspection is a relatively small step, and a high-pressure line clearing often resolves early-stage root intrusion without excavation.

For Newer Landscaping

Preventing this before it starts

If you're planting new trees or extensive landscaping and know roughly where your sewer lateral runs, choosing species with less aggressive root systems, or simply planting them further from the line, is a genuinely effective preventive step. Willows, poplars, and maples are particularly known for aggressive, water-seeking root systems, while many smaller ornamental species pose less risk. If you're not sure where your lateral runs, that's something we can help locate before you finalize new landscaping plans, rather than finding out the hard way years later.

For an already-mature landscape with trees planted decades ago near an old sewer line, prevention at this point mostly means staying attentive to the early warning signs rather than removing established trees, which usually isn't a proportionate response to the risk.

How Often Is Too Often

When repeated jetting stops being the right answer

Hydro jetting can be an effective, repeatable solution for mild, ongoing root intrusion, but if you find yourself needing it more than once a year for the same line, that's usually a sign the intrusion has progressed to the point where a more permanent fix, trenchless lining or a spot repair, makes more sense than continuing to treat the same symptom repeatedly.

If you're planning any excavation work near your property, a deck addition, a pool installation, or utility trenching, it's worth having your sewer lateral's location confirmed beforehand. Accidental damage during unrelated construction work is a separate but related cause of sewer line problems, and knowing where the line runs protects it from more than just root intrusion.

A camera inspection is also a genuinely useful step before buying a home with mature trees near the suspected sewer line path, even without any current symptoms. Root intrusion develops gradually, and catching it before it becomes a full backup gives you far more options, and far less urgency, than discovering it during an active emergency.

If you've had root intrusion addressed before and are wondering whether it will simply happen again, the honest answer is that it can, particularly if the same tree or root system remains nearby. That's part of why we're upfront about which fix fits which situation rather than defaulting to the same solution for every root intrusion call we get.
