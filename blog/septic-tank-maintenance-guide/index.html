Septic Tank Maintenance: A Homeowner's Guide for Cobb County

If your property isn't on municipal sewer, here's what actually keeps a septic system working and what shortens its life.

6 min readMarietta, GA

Most of our Marietta-area coverage is served by municipal sewer, but properties on larger lots toward the edges of the county, where municipal service doesn't reach, often rely on a septic system instead. If that's your situation, the maintenance approach is genuinely different from anything a municipal-sewer household needs to think about.

The Basics

How a septic system actually works

A septic system separates solid waste from wastewater in an underground tank, with the liquid effluent then dispersing into a drain field where soil naturally filters it. Solids settle and partially break down in the tank but still accumulate over time, which is why periodic pumping is necessary regardless of how well the system otherwise functions.

The Pumping Schedule

Why 3 to 5 years is a guideline, not a guarantee

The commonly cited 3 to 5 year pumping interval is a reasonable starting point, but household size, garbage disposal use, and water usage habits all affect how quickly a given tank actually fills. A larger household running a garbage disposal regularly will generally need more frequent pumping than a smaller household without one. If you don't know your system's specific tank size or your household's actual usage pattern, it's worth having a professional help set a realistic schedule rather than guessing based on the general rule.

What Shortens a Septic System's Life

Common habits worth avoiding

Flushing anything beyond toilet paper

Wipes, even ones labeled flushable, paper towels, and feminine hygiene products don't break down properly and can clog the system.

Excessive garbage disposal use

Food waste adds solid volume to the tank faster than a system without a disposal, shortening the interval between pumpings.

Heavy chemical drain cleaners

These can disrupt the bacterial balance in the tank that helps break down solids naturally.

Planting trees near the drain field

Root systems can infiltrate and damage drain field piping over time, similar to sewer lateral root intrusion.

Warning Signs of a Problem

What shouldn't be ignored

Slow drains throughout the house, sewage odor near the tank or drain field, and unusually lush or soggy grass over the drain field are all signs worth acting on rather than waiting out. Left unaddressed, a full tank or a struggling drain field can eventually cause a backup into the house itself, a far more disruptive and costly problem than routine pumping would have been.

If you've recently moved into a home with a septic system and don't know its maintenance history, that's a reasonable first thing to have assessed, since starting with a clear picture of the tank's current condition is better than waiting for a symptom, and if the issue turns out to be a connected sewer line rather than the tank itself, we'll tell you that too.

Drain Field Health

The part of the system you can't see is just as important

Pumping keeps the tank itself functioning, but the drain field, the underground area where treated effluent disperses into soil, is just as critical to the system's overall health. A drain field can become saturated or damaged independent of tank condition, particularly from heavy vehicle traffic over it, tree root intrusion, or simply reaching the end of its functional lifespan after many years of use. Unlike tank pumping, drain field issues are often more expensive and complicated to address, which is part of why protecting it, avoiding parking or building over it, keeping trees a reasonable distance away, matters as much as staying current on pumping.

New to Septic Ownership

What to do if you just moved into a septic property

If you've recently purchased a home with a septic system and weren't given clear maintenance records, treat that as a reasonable first priority rather than something to figure out later. An initial assessment tells you the tank's current condition, roughly when it might need pumping based on visible fill level, and whether the drain field shows any signs of existing distress, giving you a real baseline instead of an unknown system.

Cost Perspective

Why pumping is the cheap part of septic ownership

Routine pumping is a genuinely modest, predictable expense compared to what a neglected system eventually costs. A backup into the house, a failed drain field requiring replacement, or emergency pumping after a system has already backed up are all considerably more expensive and disruptive than staying current on a routine schedule. Thinking of pumping as a small, recurring cost of septic ownership, similar to changing HVAC filters, rather than an expense to delay, tends to save money over the system's lifetime.

Record Keeping

Why your own documentation matters

Keeping your own record of pumping dates, even a simple note in your phone or a home maintenance log, is worth doing regardless of whether your service provider also tracks it. If you ever sell the home, being able to hand a buyer a clear maintenance history is a genuine selling point, and if you switch service providers at some point, having your own record means you're not starting from zero.

Signs of a Failing Drain Field

Distinct from a simple full tank

A full tank and a failing drain field can produce similar symptoms, but a drain field that's genuinely failing, rather than just needing the tank pumped, often shows persistent wet or spongy ground over the field area even after pumping, or a return of symptoms shortly after a pump-out that should have resolved them for years. If pumping doesn't seem to fix the underlying issue, that's a sign to look at the drain field specifically rather than assuming another pump-out will help.
