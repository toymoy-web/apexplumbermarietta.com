Repair or Replace? A Marietta Homeowner's Guide to Water Heater Decisions

The honest answer depends mostly on one number: how old is the tank. Here's how to think through the decision either way.

6 min readMarietta, GA

Almost every water heater call we get eventually comes down to the same question: is this worth repairing, or should it just be replaced? The honest answer depends on a few factors, but the single biggest one is the age of the tank, and understanding why makes the rest of the decision much easier.

The Age Question

Why 10 years is the real dividing line

Standard tank water heaters typically last 10 to 15 years. Before that window, most failures are component-level: a thermostat, a heating element, a thermocouple, or a pressure relief valve. These are genuinely worth repairing, since the tank itself, the expensive part to replace, is still sound. Once a tank crosses into that 10 to 15 year range, though, the calculation changes. A repair on an aging tank often just delays a replacement that's coming anyway, sometimes within a year or two.

Symptoms That Point to Repair

When fixing the actual problem makes sense

Inconsistent hot water on a newer unit

Often a thermostat or heating element issue, straightforward and cost-effective to fix.

Pilot light or ignition problems on a gas unit

Usually a thermocouple or gas valve issue, not a sign the tank itself has failed.

A leak at a specific fitting or valve

A repairable connection issue, different from a leak coming from the tank body itself.

The unit is under 8 years old

Generally still within a reasonable window where repair is the more cost-effective choice.

Symptoms That Point to Replacement

When the tank itself is the problem

Rusty or discolored water from the tap

Usually internal tank corrosion, not something a repair can meaningfully fix.

Water pooling at the base of the tank

Often means the tank itself has failed internally rather than a fitting leaking.

Rumbling or popping noises

Sediment buildup at the bottom of an aging tank, a sign of accumulated wear over years.

The unit is over 10 to 12 years old with any symptom

The age alone shifts the math toward replacement even for a seemingly minor issue.

At a Glance

Repair vs. Replace

RepairReplace
Unit ageUnder 8 to 10 yearsOver 10 to 12 years

A Note on Timing

Why this decision often happens under pressure

Most water heater failures aren't gradual. A unit works fine and then suddenly doesn't, usually on a day you'd rather not deal with it. That's part of why we try to give homeowners a clear, honest read on where their tank stands during any service visit, even one for an unrelated issue, so the eventual replacement decision doesn't have to happen entirely under pressure.

If your tank is getting close to that 10-year mark, it's worth asking about its condition proactively rather than waiting for a failure. A planned replacement on your own schedule is almost always a better experience than an emergency one.

What Replacement Actually Involves

More than just swapping the tank

If you do end up needing a replacement, the visit involves more than pulling the old unit and dropping in a new one. We check the venting to confirm it still meets current requirements for the new unit, verify the water and gas or electrical connections, and size the replacement to your household's actual hot water demand rather than assuming the old tank's capacity was correct in the first place. Many older homes were fitted with an undersized tank relative to the household's actual usage, and a replacement is a natural point to correct that.

The old unit is removed and hauled away as part of the job, and most replacements are completed within the same visit for calls that come in earlier in the day, since common tank sizes are typically kept on hand.

A Quick Gut Check

Ask yourself these two questions

If you're trying to make this decision quickly, two questions usually get you most of the way there. First, is the tank itself leaking, meaning water pooling at the base, rather than a specific fitting or valve? If yes, that's a strong signal toward replacement regardless of age. Second, is the unit within the 10 to 15 year range? If it's under that window and the symptom is a specific component, repair is usually the right call. If it's at or past that range, even a seemingly minor issue is often the nudge to replace rather than delay the inevitable.

It's also worth factoring in energy efficiency when a replacement is on the table anyway. A water heater manufactured a decade or more ago is often meaningfully less efficient than a current model, which means part of the cost of replacement gets offset over time through lower utility bills, something a straightforward repair on the same old unit obviously doesn't provide.

There's no shame in wanting a second opinion on a big-ticket decision like this. If a repair estimate feels high relative to the unit's age, or a replacement recommendation feels premature for a unit that seems otherwise fine, ask directly what specifically points toward one option over the other. A straightforward answer, grounded in the tank's actual age and condition, is what you should expect from any plumber making this recommendation.

Whichever direction you go, getting a clear, itemized explanation before any work begins protects you either way, whether that's a repair invoice broken down by part and labor, or a replacement quote that specifies the exact unit, warranty terms, and what's included in installation.
