Grease Trap Maintenance: A Compliance Guide for Restaurant Owners

Beyond the clog risk, a neglected grease trap is a genuine compliance issue. Here's what Marietta-area food-service businesses need to know.

5 min readMarietta, GA

If you operate a restaurant or any food-service business in the Marietta area, your grease trap maintenance isn't just about avoiding a clogged drain, it's a genuine compliance requirement under local health and plumbing code. Here's what that actually involves in practice.

Why It's Regulated

Protecting the municipal sewer system

Grease and fats from commercial kitchens can solidify inside sewer lines, causing blockages that affect not just your own plumbing but potentially the broader municipal system downstream. That's why local health and plumbing codes typically require food-service businesses to install and maintain a grease trap or interceptor, sized and serviced according to the kitchen's specific volume.

Setting the Right Schedule

Why a generic interval doesn't work for every kitchen

Many commercial kitchens need cleaning every 1 to 3 months, but the right interval genuinely depends on your kitchen's actual grease volume, not a one-size schedule set at installation and never revisited. A high-volume fryer-heavy kitchen fills a trap faster than a lighter-volume operation, and a schedule that made sense when the trap was first installed may no longer fit if your menu or volume has changed since then.

What Health Inspectors Actually Check

Documentation matters as much as the cleaning itself

Cleaning frequency records

Inspectors typically want to see a documented, consistent cleaning history, not just evidence the trap is currently clean.

Trap condition and sizing

An undersized trap for your kitchen's volume is itself sometimes flagged, independent of how recently it was cleaned.

Proper waste disposal documentation

Grease waste requires disposal through a licensed hauler, and inspectors may ask for proof of proper disposal.

Visible signs of overflow or neglect

Grease visible in floor drains or a persistent odor near the trap area are red flags during an inspection.

The Cost of Skipping It

Cheaper to maintain than to fix after the fact

Beyond potential health code violations and fines, a neglected trap is a common cause of downstream sewer line clogs, which can mean a more disruptive and costly repair than routine cleaning would have been, on top of potential business interruption during the repair. Scheduled, documented maintenance is almost always the cheaper path over the life of the business.

If you're not sure whether your current cleaning schedule actually matches your kitchen's real volume, or if you're due for an inspection and want your documentation in order beforehand, that's exactly the kind of assessment worth having done proactively rather than reactively.

Multiple Locations

Coordinating compliance across a restaurant group

If you operate more than one location, keeping cleaning schedules and compliance documentation consistent across all of them is its own challenge, especially if different locations have different kitchen volumes or trap sizes. Working with a single service provider who tracks all your locations' schedules is often simpler than managing separate arrangements per site, and it reduces the risk of one location falling behind while others stay current.

Choosing Trap Size

Getting it right from the start

If you're opening a new location or renovating an existing kitchen, getting the grease trap sized correctly for your anticipated volume from the start avoids the more frequent cleaning schedule an undersized trap requires. This is worth discussing during the kitchen design phase rather than adjusting after the fact once the trap is already installed and undersized for actual usage.

Seasonal Volume Changes

Adjusting your schedule for busy periods

If your business has predictable high-volume seasons, holiday periods for many restaurants, for example, it's worth adjusting your grease trap cleaning schedule to account for the temporary increase in volume rather than sticking rigidly to a year-round interval that was set based on average usage. A trap that's adequate most of the year can fall behind faster during a genuinely busier stretch.

Getting Started

If you're not sure where your current schedule stands

If you've inherited a grease trap maintenance schedule from a previous owner or manager and aren't confident it actually fits your kitchen's real volume, that's a reasonable thing to have assessed rather than assuming the existing schedule is correct simply because it's already in place.

Staff Training

A simple habit that reduces grease trap strain

Beyond the trap itself, training kitchen staff to scrape excess grease and food waste into trash rather than down the drain before washing dishes meaningfully reduces the load reaching the trap in the first place. This simple habit, combined with a properly sized trap and a schedule matched to your real volume, is usually enough to keep a kitchen consistently compliant without excessive cleaning frequency.

Compliance doesn't have to be a source of stress once a consistent, appropriately sized schedule is in place. Most of the difficulty comes from an initial mismatch between schedule and actual volume, which a proper assessment resolves fairly quickly.

A quick conversation about your kitchen's actual volume is usually all it takes to get the schedule right from here forward.

Whether you're setting up a new cleaning schedule or fixing one that's clearly not keeping pace with your kitchen, we're happy to walk through the specifics as part of our broader commercial plumbing work before your next visit.

Cleaning schedules aside, proper waste disposal documentation is worth keeping organized in one place, since it's often the very first thing an inspector asks to see, before even looking at the trap itself.

A little organization here goes a long way toward making inspections routine rather than stressful, regardless of how your kitchen's volume happens to be trending that particular quarter.
