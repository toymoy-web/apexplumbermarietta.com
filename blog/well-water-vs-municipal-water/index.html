Well Water vs. Municipal Water: What Marietta-Area Well Owners Should Know

A small number of properties in our coverage area rely on private wells. Here's what's genuinely different about maintaining that kind of system.

5 min readMarietta, GA

Most of our Marietta-area service area runs on municipal water, but a smaller number of properties, typically on larger, more rural lots at the edges of our coverage, rely on a private well instead. If that's your situation, the maintenance responsibilities and potential issues are genuinely different from what a municipal-water household deals with.

The Core Difference

You own the entire system, not just the pipes

With municipal water, the utility owns and maintains everything up to your meter. With a well, you own the entire system: the well itself, the pump, the pressure tank, and all associated equipment. That means issues that would be the utility's responsibility on a municipal connection become yours to diagnose and repair on a well system.

Common Well System Issues

What actually goes wrong

Pressure tank problems

A waterlogged tank or failed pressure switch causes pulsing or cycling pressure, more common than an actual pump failure.

Pump age and wear

Most submersible well pumps last 10 to 15 years, with water quality and usage affecting that range.

Sediment or cloudiness

Can indicate well or pump condition issues, sometimes requiring assessment beyond the pump itself.

A well running low during dry periods

Extended dry spells can genuinely reduce well output, showing up as reduced water or a pump straining without adequate water.

Water Quality Considerations

A responsibility municipal customers don't have

Municipal water is regularly tested and treated to meet regulatory standards. Well water isn't subject to the same oversight, which means periodic testing is genuinely the homeowner's responsibility rather than something happening automatically in the background. This isn't something we handle directly, well water quality testing typically goes through a certified testing lab, but it's worth building into your maintenance routine if you're on a private well.

When to Call a Plumber vs. a Well Specialist

The line isn't always obvious

Pressure tank, pressure switch, and general plumbing issues connected to a well system are within our scope. Well drilling, well depth issues, and water quality testing typically involve a specialized well contractor or testing lab instead. If you're not sure which category your issue falls into, describing the symptom is enough for us to point you in the right direction, even if the answer is that you need someone else for part of it.

Seasonal Considerations

Why dry summers matter more for well owners

Municipal water customers rarely think about drought conditions affecting their water supply directly, but a well owner genuinely can, since well output depends on the local water table, which drops during extended dry periods. If you're on a well and notice reduced pressure or output specifically during a dry summer stretch, that's worth distinguishing from a pump or pressure tank failure, since the underlying cause and the appropriate response are different.

Buying a Home With a Well

What to check before you close

If you're purchasing a property with a well, it's worth having both the water quality tested by a certified lab and the pump system's condition assessed before closing, similar in spirit to a home inspection for the rest of the property. A well system with unknown age and unknown maintenance history is a real unknown cost to inherit, and understanding its condition upfront is far better than discovering an issue shortly after moving in.

Backup Planning

What happens if your well pump fails entirely

Unlike a municipal water outage, which is the utility's problem to fix, a well pump failure means your entire household water supply stops until it's repaired. For this reason, well owners benefit from knowing our emergency contact information ahead of time, rather than searching for a plumber during an actual no-water emergency, and from understanding roughly how quickly a pump repair or replacement can typically be completed.

The Upside

Wells aren't only a source of extra responsibility

It's worth noting that well ownership isn't purely a downside compared to municipal service. Many well owners appreciate not having a monthly water bill tied to usage, and a well-maintained system, with a pump checked regularly and filtration added where needed, can run reliably for many years with relatively modest ongoing costs.

Emergency Preparedness

What every well-owning household should have on hand

Keeping a small backup supply of drinking water on hand is a reasonable precaution for any well-owning household, given that a pump failure means no water at all until it's repaired, rather than the reduced pressure or intermittent service a municipal customer might experience during a utility issue. This isn't meant to cause alarm, well systems are generally reliable, but a modest backup supply costs little and provides real peace of mind.

Whatever your specific well setup looks like, staying proactive about pressure tank checks and pump age is the single most effective thing you can do to avoid an unexpected total loss of water at an inconvenient time.

A brief annual check, even without an active complaint, tends to catch small issues well before they become a total loss-of-water situation.

If it's been more than a year since anyone looked at your pressure tank, switch, or pump condition, that alone is a reasonable enough reason to schedule a check, even without an active symptom prompting it.
